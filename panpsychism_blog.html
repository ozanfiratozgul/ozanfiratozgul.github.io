Panpsychism: A Curious Perspective on Consciousness

By Ozan Fırat Özgül | June 2024

DISCUSSIONS ON THE HARD PROBLEM OF CONSCIOUSNESS

The brain's mechanisms for integrating and processing information remain unresolved, capturing the attention of researchers and philosophers for decades. While definitive answers are yet to be found, David Chalmers refers to such problems as ‘easy’. This label arises because these processes are well-defined and lie within the limits of our explanatory power. For example, we can measure the brain’s response to specific stimuli in experimental conditions and fit hypothetical neural models to the observed data. If the model successfully explains and predicts outcomes, the problem is considered solved, and researchers can shift focus to other easy problems. However, not all questions about our brains are equally straightforward.

Addressing questions about our conscious experiences does not align with this clear-cut methodology. While we can gain insights into the processes contributing to our mental states, these insights fail to explain our subjective conscious experiences. Every moment of our lives resembles a scene from a movie, composed of countless perceptions, feelings, and experiences. Even if we achieve a complete understanding of neuronal processes, this will not bridge the ‘explanatory gap’ between physical neural activity and the rich tapestry of our conscious experiences.

DOES THE HARD PROBLEM EXIST? STRONG REDUCTIONISM

The strong reductionist approach claims that the hard problem is not a problem at all. According to this view, conscious experiences, while undeniable, are emergent results of solving easy problems. Therefore, unraveling conscious experiences requires only an understanding of the underlying neuronal activities—or more precisely, the material brain.

This approach offers both scientific and philosophical benefits. First, it breaks down a complex and seemingly insurmountable question into smaller, more manageable problems—a classical scientific strategy. This decomposition empowers neuroscience research by offering tangible goals and measurable outcomes. Furthermore, strong reductionism aligns with the historical success of science in explaining complex phenomena through incremental discovery.

CONSCIOUSNESS AS A DISTINCT SUBSTANCE: PANPSYCHISM

When science struggles to provide answers, it’s natural to explore alternative frameworks. Panpsychism is one such perspective, and while I don’t find it entirely convincing, it remains intellectually stimulating and worth exploring.

Panpsychism posits that consciousness is a fundamental property of the physical world—ubiquitous across all matter. In this view, even seemingly inanimate objects possess some rudimentary level of consciousness. As Chalmers suggests, even a rock could have inner subjective experiences, albeit vastly different from ours.

INFORMATION INTEGRATION THEORY (IIT)

IIT proposes that an object’s level of consciousness scales with its capacity to process and integrate information. This idea feels both intuitive and scientifically grounded:

  • It avoids arbitrary thresholds.
  • It aligns with our intuition that systems with richer information flows exhibit richer consciousness.
  • It builds upon measurable, mathematical principles derived from information theory.

CLOSING REMARKS

In this paper, I introduced the hard problem of consciousness, examined it through the lens of strong reductionism, and explored the alternative perspective of panpsychism. While I lean towards a reductionist view, I recognize the appeal of frameworks like IIT in addressing aspects of the hard problem.

Ultimately, I believe that our understanding of consciousness will deepen as we continue to unravel the simpler components of brain function. With time, I hope scientific advances will bridge the explanatory gap, sparing us from relying on overly speculative frameworks to explain something as profoundly human as consciousness.
