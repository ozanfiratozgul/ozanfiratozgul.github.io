Discussions on the Hard Problem of Consciousness

By Ozan Fırat Özgül | June 2024

The brain's mechanisms for integrating and processing information remain unresolved, capturing the attention of researchers and philosophers for decades. While definitive answers are yet to be found, David Chalmers refers to such problems as ‘easy’. This label arises because these processes are well-defined and lie within the limits of our explanatory power.

However, not all questions about our brains are equally straightforward. Addressing questions about our conscious experiences does not align with this clear-cut methodology. This challenge constitutes the ‘hard problem of consciousness’ and can be distilled into two fundamental questions:

  • What is the relationship between physical occurrences (neuronal activity) and mental states (consciousness)?
  • Why do physical processes generate conscious experiences?

Does the Hard Problem Exist? Strong Reductionism

The strong reductionist approach claims that the hard problem is not a problem at all. According to this view, conscious experiences are emergent results of solving easy problems. Therefore, unraveling conscious experiences requires only an understanding of the underlying neuronal activities—or more precisely, the material brain.

This approach offers scientific and philosophical benefits by breaking complex questions into smaller, more manageable problems—a classical scientific strategy. However, accepting the hard problem as unsolvable introduces significant challenges:

  • Consciousness risks being framed as inherently unknowable.
  • Suggesting that consciousness transcends scientific inquiry risks abandoning science instead of advancing it.

Consciousness as a Distinct Substance: Panpsychism

When science struggles to provide answers, it’s natural to explore alternative frameworks. Panpsychism posits that consciousness is a fundamental property of the physical world—ubiquitous across all matter.

Some proponents argue for a consciousness threshold, where only systems surpassing a certain point exhibit complex subjective experiences. Information Integration Theory (IIT) refines this by suggesting that consciousness scales with the degree of information integration in a system.

However, IIT is not without flaws. For example, mammals share similar sensory and cognitive architectures, yet exhibit different levels of consciousness. Additionally, our intuitions about consciousness are often biased by physical characteristics, such as motion or temperature.

Closing Remarks

In this paper, I introduced the hard problem of consciousness, examined it through the lens of strong reductionism, and explored the alternative perspective of panpsychism. While I lean towards a reductionist view, I recognize the appeal of frameworks like IIT in addressing aspects of the hard problem.

With time, I hope scientific advances will bridge the explanatory gap, sparing us from relying on overly speculative frameworks to explain something as profoundly human as consciousness.
